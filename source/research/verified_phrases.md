# Verified Russian Phrases — Meeting the In-Laws (St. Petersburg family dinner)

**Learner:** English-speaking husband (reads Cyrillic). His wife's parents are his **тесть** (father-in-law) and **тёща** (mother-in-law). He, to them, is the **зять** (son-in-law).

**How to read this:** stress marks (U+0301 combining acute, e.g. о́/а́/е́/и́) are on every multi-syllable word. Pronunciation hints appear only where tricky (unstressed о→[a], г→[v] in -ого/-его, soft consonants). Gender notes flag forms a **male** speaker must use. Confidence: HIGH = confirmed in 2+ independent sources; MED = one solid source / standard but unconfirmed by a second; LOW = could not fully confirm → **rehearse with Kadriya**.

**Sources keyed:** `LRSBS` = learnrussianstepbystep.com (scraped); `PLA` = Polyglottist Language Academy toasts article (scraped); `GW2RU-rel` = gw2ru.com relatives explainer (scraped); `GW2RU-toast` = gw2ru.com "Na zdorovye is not a toast" (scraped); `Wikt` = Wiktionary (per-word stress); `RL` = russianlessons.net; `etiq` = etiquettescholar / italki / bestrussianfood (etiquette, 2+ agreeing). "+2 sources" means cross-checked against the search-result corpus.

## ⚠️ READ FIRST — three things a generic phrasebook gets wrong

1. **«На здоро́вье» is NOT a toast.** It is a reply to *thank you* (≈ "you're welcome," especially for food/drink). Saying it as "cheers" is a Hollywood myth (likely from Polish *Na zdrowie!*). To toast *to health*, say **«За здоро́вье!»** or **«За ва́ше здоро́вье!»**. *(GW2RU-toast, +3 sources. The LRSBS source mislabels it — ignore that label.)*
2. **тесть / тёща are referential, not how you address them.** You will rarely call them "тесть/тёща" to their faces — those are words *they and others* use to describe the relationship (*«наш зя́ть»* = "our son-in-law"). You address elders with **и́мя + о́тчество** (first name + patronymic) and **вы**. → **Learn their patronymics in advance — rehearse with Kadriya.**
3. **юри́ст vs адвока́т.** **адвока́т** specifically = courtroom advocate / bar member (like a barrister). For a general US attorney, the safe self-description is **«Я юри́ст»**. *(+2 sources)*

## CULTURAL NOTES (verified)

- **Flowers (if bringing some):** must be an **ODD** number (3, 5, 7…). An **even** number is for **funerals** only — do not invert this. *(italki, bestrussianfood, +1)*
- **Shoes off at the door.** Russians remove shoes when entering a home; hosts usually offer **та́почки** ([tá-pach-ki], slippers). *(reddit AskARussian, +2)*
- **Bring a gift:** flowers for the тёща, plus something like wine/good chocolate/a treat is well received. *(etiquette sources, MED)*
- **Address elders formally:** **вы** + **и́мя + о́тчество** (first name + patronymic) until invited to use ты. → Confirm both parents' patronymics ahead of time. *(Wikipedia, +2)*
- **Toasting etiquette:**
  - Make **eye contact** during the toast and the clink — signals sincerity. *(etiquettescholar, trykaiwa, +1)*
  - **Standing** is for honorific or memorial toasts (e.g. to someone's memory, or a formal tribute); at a casual family dinner most toasts are made **seated** — don't pop up for every glass. *(etiq, MED)*
  - At a happy family dinner you **DO clink glasses.** The "**не чока́ются**" (no-clinking) rule applies to toasts for the **dead / at funerals/memorials** — so clinking here is correct and expected. *(toast literature, +2)*
  - **«До дна́»** (drain the glass) is **not** required every time — reserve full drinking for emphatic/special toasts; pacing yourself is respected. *(PLA "moderation," etiq)*
  - Eat a bite (bread, a pickle, zakuski) after a vodka shot — expected, not impolite. *(etiquettescholar)*
  - Don't refuse the *first* toast outright; even a small sip honors it.
- **«Го́рько!»** ([gór'-ka], "bitter!") is a **wedding** shout for the couple to kiss — handy to recognize, but not for a dinner. *(PLA)*
